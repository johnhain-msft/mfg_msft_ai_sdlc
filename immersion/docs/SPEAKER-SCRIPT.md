### [SLIDE 2] · m1-vibe-cliff · Greenfield demos hit a cliff

> **Presenter:** John Hain · **Module:** M1
> **Purpose:** Show why a flawless greenfield demo predicts little about brownfield results.
> **The room sees:** Greenfield demos hit a cliff (The scaling cliff)
> - 2 cards: Greenfield demo, 10M-line brownfield
> - 1 band: Three structural properties
> - Footnote: † Handbook author estimate; no controlled study

Take any greenfield demo. The agent builds a clean service in minutes, because there is no history to trip over. Point the same model at a codebase with years of decisions in it, and the experience changes.

The Agentic SDLC Handbook, written by Daniel Meppiel at Microsoft in his personal capacity, calls this the vibe coding cliff. The thirty-to-sixty percent rework figure on the slide is the author's own synthesis, and the Handbook says no controlled study has settled on a single number. Treat it as a ballpark and measure your own repos.

Why does it happen? Three structural reasons, and none is a bug a vendor patches next quarter. Attention is finite and fragile. The model only uses what is explicitly in front of it, so a convention that lives in a senior engineer's head doesn't exist for the agent. And the output is a draw from a distribution, so the same prompt can give different diffs on different days.

Picture a rule your best architect never wrote down, say, never call that internal service synchronously. The agent will happily do it, and everything compiles.

### [SLIDE 3] · m1-attention-economy · Window size is not focus

> **Presenter:** John Hain · **Module:** M1
> **Purpose:** Establish that a bigger context window does not mean more of it is in focus.
> **The room sees:** Window size is not focus (The scaling cliff)
> - 1 card: Liu et al., TACL 2024
> - 1 band: Five PROSE constraints
> - A diagram: Accuracy by position in the context window: highest at the head and tail, lowest in the middle trough. Three PROSE levers lift the trough.
> **Interactive:** Space or → steps the highlight through 3 stages before moving on; click a stage to jump to it.

The context window is how much the model can hold, not how much it's paying attention to. Liu and colleagues made that measurable. They took long inputs, in multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval, and moved the one relevant fact around.

Accuracy followed position. It was best when the fact sat at the beginning or the end, and it fell significantly when the fact was buried mid-input, even for models built for long context. Chapter 15 of the Handbook connects that middle drop-off to context rot as a long session fills up.

Translate that to a coding session. This is an illustration, not a measurement: your standing instructions load at the head, your current task sits at the tail, and everything you drag in between competes for the weak part of the curve.

> **[CUE]** CLICK: Press Space three times as you name the levers. Each press lifts the middle of the curve: progressive disclosure first, then reduced scope, then orchestrated composition.

PROSE is the Handbook's answer. Three of its five constraints are direct levers on attention. Progressive disclosure means loading the instructions for the module you are touching and nothing else. Reduced scope means smaller tasks. Orchestrated composition means chaining small, single-purpose primitives and handing sub-tasks to fresh agents with clean windows. The other two, safety boundaries and explicit hierarchy, keep that discipline safe and predictable.

This is how a lot of programs start. Two or three of the strongest architects and developers build V1. That works, and it's why so many organizations have a working V1 at all.

The Handbook names the risk on the far side of that success: the hero pilot. A few top people, a friendly problem, brilliant results, and nothing they learned transfers. In practice, that usually means it lives in their heads instead of in shared assets.

> **[CUE]** CLICK: Press Space to step the ladder: hero pilot, then enabling CoE, then advisory guardrails. Leave each rung lit while you talk about it.

What the Handbook describes instead is two small groups. An enabling team with a finite lifespan that guides other groups and writes practices down. And a small central group that encodes the cost-effective workflows everyone else reuses. Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework says much the same from the other direction. Fold AI expertise into an existing center of excellence where you can. Start centralized early, so you build reusable assets and avoid fragmented adoption. Then move to an advisory guardrail role, so the group never turns into the approval choke point.

How long? At least six to twelve months is the realistic enterprise window. That fits the enabling-team idea: dedicated focus while practices take root, with a planned shift to advisory guardrails.

## Module M3: Metric evolution & evaluation frameworks

### [SLIDE 8] · m3-productivity-paradox · Faster coding is not faster delivery

> **Presenter:** John Hain · **Module:** M3
> **Purpose:** Explain why a headline coding-speed gain overstates end-to-end delivery gains.
> **The room sees:** Faster coding is not faster delivery (Metrics & evals)
> - 3 cards: Denominator problem, Quality discount, Attribution problem
> - A diagram: Two bars to the same scale: a 50% reduction in coding time against the 15% it saves overall
> - Footnote: Lab finding: 55.8% less time on one JavaScript task

The number you've probably seen is 55 percent faster. It comes from a real lab study, Peng and colleagues, 2023, where developers finished one JavaScript HTTP-server task in 55.8 percent less time. It's a good study, but it's one task in a lab.

The Handbook's point is that if you hand that to a board as "we are 55 percent faster," it falls apart in three places. First, the denominator. If coding is about thirty percent of a developer's week, halving it buys back roughly fifteen percent. That's still worth having. It just isn't fifty-five percent.

Second, the quality discount. Speed that arrives as rework isn't speed, and the thirty-to-sixty percent rework estimate I quoted earlier is the author's own estimate, with no controlled study behind it. Third, attribution. The developer prompts, the agent writes, the developer edits and prompts again. Credit the AI and you overstate its contribution; credit the developer and you understate the tool.

The root cause is that we treat code as the output. Code is an intermediate artifact, and until it ships I'd call it inventory. So I'd judge return on investment in an agentic SDLC by whether we delivered valuable software faster, at lower unit cost, with equal or better quality, reliability, security, and developer experience.

DORA's 2025 research frames AI as an amplifier of an organization's existing strengths and weaknesses. AI lifts throughput, but it keeps putting pressure on delivery stability unless automated testing, version control, fast feedback loops, and loosely coupled architecture hold the line.

I'll walk through the usual pattern for how companies pick their first AI metrics, find out they're flawed or gamed, and change course. Then I'd like to hear where your own dashboards sit.

Most teams start with what's easy to count, because the tool emits it: seats activated, suggestions accepted, lines generated. GitHub's own usage-metrics documentation says these numbers show association, not that the tool caused a productivity gain.

> **[CUE]** CLICK: Press Space once per stage. The stage you are talking about lifts and the other two step back.

The second stage moves to flow and confidence. Separate creation time from elapsed delivery time. Cutting coding from eight hours to four creates little value if the work still spends nine days waiting for review and deployment. Time-to-confident-merge is the one I'd start with: how long until a reviewer approves without reservations. Then pair speed with quality metrics like rejection rate, thirty-day rework rate, and code survival, meaning how much generated code is still there at sixty and ninety days. Generated code that has to be corrected, secured, operated, and rewritten is a liability.

The third stage gives leadership numbers it can use, like cost per outcome, escaped defects, and how often a person has to step in. Don't just count the interventions. Ask why each one happened. If a person stepped in because policy requires a security approval, that's a healthy control, not an agent failure. If they stepped in because of an unplanned failure, like a hallucinated API or a broken implementation, that's rework. Golden-dataset evaluations belong in this stage too, and Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework recommends them so quality is tested against known-good answers.

This one is a hypothetical, not a named customer. A team launches with a leaderboard for accepted suggestions and lines generated. Within weeks, pull requests balloon because developers accept verbose boilerplate, and senior reviewers drown in subtle defects. Swap the leaderboard for review rejection and thirty-day rework, and the incentive flips toward smaller diffs and grounded specs.

> **Interactive:** Press 1–4 (or click a chip) to pick a failure; the matching gate tiles ring. Press 0 to clear.

The Handbook gives us a simple two-by-two for quality gates. One axis is who renders the verdict: something inside the agent's own process, or something outside it. The other is how: a programmatic check, or a judgment call.

As the Handbook puts it, if the failure mode you fear is goal drift, the test suite is theater. Tests green, lint clean, and the agent built the wrong feature. A programmatic gate inside the run verifies form, not intent.

So pick the gate by the failure. A schema break wants a schema validator. An invented fact, say an issue filed for a customer that doesn't exist, wants a grounded lookup against the system of record, not a reviewer reading prose. Goal drift wants a judgment gate: a goal-steward re-reading the spec, or a human checkpoint at the design step. And when an agent says the tests pass, don't take its word. Re-run the suite in a separate pipeline, check the git diff, or score the output with an external rubric.

The lesson from many first versions is that tests passing inside an agent turn cannot catch goal drift or skipped tests; golden-fixture evals plus an independent reviewer close the gap.

## Module M4: Process economics & prompt optimization

### [SLIDE 11] · m4-process-economics · Can we build it? Should we?

> **Presenter:** John Hain · **Module:** M4
> **Purpose:** Reframe AI cost as a business-process question before a token question.
> **The room sees:** Can we build it? Should we? (Process economics)
> - 2 cards: Token-only view, Business-process view
> - 1 band: Baseline three processes
> - A diagram: A balance: the token bill on one pan, the whole business process on the heavier pan

You've all heard that AI lets us build software faster than ever. True. But just because we can build it faster, should we? Engineering activity isn't the return. The return shows up when improved engineering performance changes a business outcome.

Most cost conversations stop at the model call, tokens in and tokens out. That's easy to measure, and it's the wrong denominator. A business-process view starts with what the process costs today in human hours and in calendar delay, the weeks a requirement sits waiting for an answer.

Microsoft's business-value guidance starts by defining success metrics, unit value, and a baseline before you build. In software delivery, we recommend measuring that value in concrete outcome units: a feature released, a vulnerability remediated, an incident resolved, an application migrated, or a customer workflow automated. Then ask three questions. What does this process cost now? What would it cost with AI in it? What is the outcome worth?

Aim at the highest friction first: a requirements intake that takes weeks, a legacy analysis nobody wants to start, QA triage that eats a senior engineer's Monday. Those are illustrations of a typical enterprise profile, not measurements from your own organization.

### [SLIDE 12] · m4-executive-roi · Measuring executive ROI

> **Presenter:** John Hain · **Module:** M4
> **Purpose:** Give executives a six-part ROI scorecard, unit-cost formula, and trace protocol.
> **The room sees:** Measuring executive ROI (Process economics)
> - 1 band: ROI & trace
> - A table of 6 rows (Category, Executive measure)
> - A diagram: End-to-end trace from business request to production outcome feeding the net ROI ratio

For an executive review, I wouldn't walk in with one universal productivity number. Microsoft Research's SPACE framework and GitHub's metrics guidance both warn that no single metric captures engineering productivity. Our full measurement guide covers nine engineering areas: business outcomes, end-to-end flow, quality and rework, reliability and operations, security and governance, agent effectiveness, developer experience, adoption and maturity, and total cost and unit economics. Those roll up into the six executive scorecard categories on this slide.

On the People row, combine telemetry with short pulse surveys on focus, cognitive load, and review fatigue. And never use agent telemetry to evaluate individual developers. If agents save authoring time but developers spend the savings verifying low-confidence output, all we've done is move the work.

On the Economics row, Microsoft's agent ROI guidance warns against the time-savings trap: claiming financial value from theoretical time savings with no chain of evidence to business outcomes. In practice, saved hours only become financial return when they're redirected to additional outcomes, lower external spend, or avoided hiring, or when you intentionally reinvest them in tests, docs, security, and technical debt.

A practical way to calculate net ROI is to take verified value created plus verified cost avoided, subtract total agentic operating cost, and divide by that same operating cost. Then pair it with FinOps unit economics, where you pick a unit and cost it. For delivery, that could be a completed story, an accepted pull request, a release, a resolved defect or incident, or a migrated application. Token cost alone isn't agent cost. The denominator in that formula has to include licenses, hosting, integration, human review, retries, governance, and maintenance.

To make those numbers hold up in a boardroom, I'd do three things. First, capture a baseline of four to eight weeks of data, or the ninety days Microsoft Learn recommends. Segment it by work complexity, repository, team, stack, legacy versus greenfield, and risk tier.

Second, run a pilot cohort and a comparison cohort on one or two repeatable workflows, like unit-test generation, vulnerability remediation, defect triage, dependency upgrades, docs, incident diagnosis, or Playwright validation. Test an initial target, say a twenty-to-thirty percent cycle-time improvement, with zero quality or compliance loss. Don't scale from a demo or a naive before-and-after.

Third, instrument one trace ID from business request through work item, agent run, model and tool calls, commit, pull request, tests and evaluations, deployment, and production result.

### [SLIDE 14] · m4-cost-spread · The same task, an 8.5× bill

> **Presenter:** John Hain · **Module:** M4
> **Purpose:** Show that identical work can differ 8.5 times in cost by workflow design.
> **The room sees:** The same task, an 8.5× bill (Process economics)
> - 2 cards: Undesigned loop $41.01, Designed loop $4.81
> - 1 band: Identical output
> - A diagram: Two bars drawn to scale, $41.01 and $4.81, joined by a bracket because the output is identical

Take one ordinary task: refactor nineteen files to adopt a new error-handling convention. Run it two ways. In the first, the agent grinds through the files one at a time on a frontier model, re-reading context, re-planning, and retrying after every failure. In the second, a designed workflow plans once, batches the edits, sends the mechanical work to a cheaper model, and verifies at the end.

Nineteen files either way, and the tests pass either way. In the Handbook's illustrative run, one path cost forty-one dollars and one cent, the other four dollars and eighty-one cents: an 8.5-times spread, or 88.3 percent lower on the designed path. The Handbook says these are single runs at point-in-time prices, not benchmarks. Treat them as an existence proof that variance can be engineered, not as a promised saving.

Your AI bill is more a variance problem than a price problem, and the Handbook borrows portfolio thinking for it: you don't manage a portfolio by its average, you manage it by its tail. A designed workflow bounds your worst case to the worst planned step. An undesigned one bounds it to nothing.

Can't every developer just optimize their own prompts? The Handbook says no, for structural reasons. Nobody should have to be a cost expert, nobody re-derives the cheap path on every prompt, and you can't switch harness per task. So you engineer it once, centrally, and everyone reuses the result. A small central AI team on gated frontier models codes the loops, and everyone else runs them on baseline tiers. That's the cost-effective-loop factory.

This is the model I use to explain why the gap exists. It's mine, it's illustrative, and I tuned it so that with everything switched on it lands near the Handbook's run. Treat it as a teaching device, not a price quote. Flip the toggles and watch the bar.

Toggle one is the context. A flat dump of thousands of lines loads on every turn whether or not it is relevant. Loading only what the step needs is progressive disclosure, and it trims the tokens you pay for on every call.

Toggle four is models and turns. The Handbook calls routing mechanical work to a frontier model the most common way teams overpay. A gradient workflow gives each stage the smallest model class it needs, a heavy planner and a mid-tier executor, and a retry or turn budget stops the loop from burning money.

### [SLIDE 20] · m6-two-computers · Two computers, one program

> **Presenter:** John Hain · **Module:** M6
> **Purpose:** Establish the seam where model proposals meet deterministic gates.
> **The room sees:** Two computers, one program (Guardrails)
> - 2 bands: Strong-form supervision, Where it exists today
> - A diagram: The seam: a probabilistic model proposes, then schema, allowlist and audit-log checks sit on the seam before the deterministic side disposes. A rejected proposal returns to the model.

Chapter 16 of the Handbook says the placement of the seam, not the choice of model, not the size of the context window, not the elegance of the prompt, is the single most important architectural decision in any agentic design. The model proposes; the gate disposes.

Think of two computers running in lockstep. The deterministic one is the machine you already know how to run: tests, lockfiles, API calls, audit logs. When it fails, it fails loudly. The probabilistic one is the model. When it fails, it fails quietly, in the Handbook's words: confident, plausible, wrong. Anything consequential has to happen on the deterministic side.

Strong-form supervised execution makes that real. The substrate denies the agent direct authority over protected effects like merging to trunk or shipping to production. The agent emits a structured proposal, and a deterministic stage validates the schema, checks an allowlist, logs the audit trail, and applies the effect. Weak-form is a prompt asking the agent to behave, which the Handbook considers adequate on a laptop, where a bad step is easy to revert, but not for anything consequential.

> **[CUE]** POINT: Hover or tab onto a station on the seam to light it: schema, allowlist, audit log. The red arrow is a rejected proposal going back to the model.

Two real implementations show the two patterns. First, branch-scoped writes without merge authority. GitHub's Copilot cloud agent runs with restricted credentials and pushes only to one permitted branch, either a new copilot-prefixed branch or the existing pull request's branch. It cannot mark its draft ready, approve, or merge. Second, a read-only agent with separate execution jobs. GitHub Agentic Workflows has safe-outputs, where the agent job is read-only and emits structured requests, and separate permission-controlled jobs execute what is permitted, gated on human review or run under policy.

The Handbook's illustration is an agent that filed an issue for a customer that didn't exist, because it held the write token and nothing checked the name. A better model wouldn't have fixed that; a gate would have.
